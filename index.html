What is this platform?

An interactive orientation programme built on ICAO Doc 9137 Airport Services Manual, Part 3, Wildlife Hazard Management (5th edition, 2020). It is designed for airport operators, wildlife controllers, safety managers and aviation professionals.

10 modules covering the Doc 9137 chapters, with tables, flow diagrams and risk matrices
Chapter assessments (60% to pass) with a clause reference for every answer
A risk calculator and a full method guide for Chapter 3
A final master exam and a downloadable completion certificate

This is a training tool. It does not replace your aerodrome's own Wildlife Hazard Management Programme or a site-specific assessment by competent personnel.

Why does it exist?

Doc 9137 states that the objective of wildlife hazard management is to reduce the risk of a wildlife strike by implementing appropriate mitigation measures (Clause 1.3.1).

It also explains that the risk depends on the size, behaviour and number of wildlife and on how close they are to aircraft in each phase of flight (Clause 1.1.2). Because traffic, engine design and land use keep changing, the manual calls for regular review of the programme (Clause 1.1.3).

This platform makes that framework easier to learn, apply and check, for people who may not have formal access to aviation safety training.

Risk calculator guide

What it does and what to expect

What it is

A screening calculator for the method in Chapter 3 of ICAO Doc 9137 Part 3. For each species it estimates how likely a strike is (probability) and how bad it would be (severity), then combines them: risk = probability x severity (Clause 3.1.4).

How to use it

  1. 1Describe the species. Name it, then pick its body mass band and flock behaviour. The quick chips fill these from the Doc 9137 examples.
  2. 2Add what you know. Days seen per year and strikes per year (or simple ratings). Optional: damage history, and a professional judgement override with a reason.
  3. 3Read the live result. It updates as you type. Open "Show the working" to see every indicator and the rule applied.
  4. 4Add it to the register. Repeat for other species. The register ranks them, plots them on the matrix, and exports to CSV.

What you will get

  • Probability and severity on the five-level scale (very low to very high).
  • A risk level: Level 1 Acceptable (green), Level 2 Tolerable (yellow), Level 3 Intolerable (red), with the Clause 3.4.3 action.
  • Three suggested next steps linked to the relevant chapters.
  • Checks that flag inconsistent or missing data.
  • A ranked register and matrix showing where to act first.

Your data

Where it is saved, and how to keep it safe

Where

In your browser, on this device, in what the browser calls local storage. It is not uploaded anywhere. StopBIRDSTRIKE cannot see your register or your WHMP draft, and cannot recover them for you.

How

The page saves automatically every time you add, edit or remove a species. It is still there after you close the tab or restart the device.

It can disappear if you

  • clear your browsing data, cookies or "site data";
  • use a private or incognito window (everything is erased when it closes);
  • open the platform in a different browser, profile or device (it will not be there);
  • use a shared or office computer that wipes itself or restores a clean profile;
  • press Log out and reset;
  • let the browser tidy up: some browsers, notably Safari, may remove site data if you do not visit for a while.

How to keep it safe

  • Export a JSON backup after every working session. It is a small file with all your species, reasons and dates.
  • Keep the file somewhere safe: a cloud drive, or email it to yourself.
  • Import the file to restore your work on this or any other device, or send it to a colleague so they can import it and continue.
  • Save a PDF report for your records.
  • Do not rely on the browser alone for an important assessment.

    Method guide ICAO Doc 9137 Part 3 • Chapter 3

    How the risk is calculated

    A plain-language, clause-by-clause walk through the method in Chapter 3 of ICAO Doc 9137 Part 3, and exactly how the risk calculator applies it.

    How to read this guide

    In plain words

    Short explanations in everyday language.

    Doc 9137 says

    What the manual states, with the clause number so you can check it. Paraphrased, not copied.

    Calculator choice

    A place where the calculator had to make a decision that Doc 9137 does not make for you. These are listed again in section 9.

    Doc 9137 gives its tables as examples. Each aerodrome should set its own scales, because the damage a strike causes also depends on the aircraft that operate there (Clause 3.3.10). Treat the numbers here as a worked method, not as limits that apply everywhere.

    1 The big picture

    In plain words

    An aerodrome cannot control every animal at once. A risk assessment tells you which species to deal with first. For each species you ask two questions: how likely is it to hit an aircraft? and how bad would it be if it did? Multiply the answers and you get the risk.

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.1.1 Assessments let potentially scarce wildlife control resources be aimed at the most important areas.
    • Clause 3.1.4 A hazard is a condition or object that could cause or contribute to an aircraft incident or accident. Here, the hazard is the presence of certain wildlife on or near an aerodrome. Safety risk is the predicted probability and severity of the consequences of a hazard. For wildlife, it is the probability of a strike by a particular species multiplied by the severity of damage to the aircraft that might reasonably occur.
    • Clause 3.1.5 Strike databases worldwide show a consistent relationship between wildlife mass and the percentage of damage to aircraft. A strike by a flock of a species is more likely to damage the aircraft than a strike by a single bird of that species. The larger the bird and the more it tends to be struck in groups, the greater the risk.
    • Clause 3.1.6 Estimating how likely a strike is for a given population is harder than estimating the harm, because wildlife behaviour cannot be predicted with certainty.

    2 Step 1 and 2: the area, and what you need to know

    In plain words

    Decide where you are looking: the whole aerodrome and what surrounds it, with special attention to the approach and take-off paths where aircraft are low. Then find out what lives and moves there. You cannot rate a species you have not recorded.

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.1.2 The first step is to define the area to be assessed. It should include the entire aerodrome and its vicinity, in particular aircraft approach and take-off.
    • Clause 3.1.3 Knowing the wildlife, its movements and what attracts it is essential. You get this from an adequate wildlife monitoring programme and by keeping historical records.
    • Clause 1.3.3 To manage the hazard you first assess the level of risk for each species present, so presence, strikes and near misses are recorded at species level.
    • Clause 1.3.4 Four kinds of event should be recorded: a collision with evidence (remains or damage), a collision with no evidence but a clear indication (seen or heard), wildlife found dead on the aerodrome without another obvious cause, and wildlife presence that could affect a flight, such as a missed approach or aborted take-off.

    3 Step 3: probability

    In plain words

    Probability is your best estimate of how often a species will be in the wrong place at the wrong time. It comes from two kinds of evidence: what has already happened (strikes and incidents) and what you see now (how often the species is present, where, and how it behaves).

    3.1 What goes into it

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.2.1 Probability should be calculated from wildlife incident data (the events in Clause 1.3.4) together with current data on the presence, location and behaviour of wildlife. Strike records also show daily and seasonal trends.
    • Clause 3.2.4 The more you know about the presence and behaviour of wildlife on and around the aerodrome, the stronger the estimate and the final risk assessment.

    3.2 When data are thin

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.2.2 Aerodromes with fewer operations may generate fewer collisions, so strike data alone may not allow accurate or useful prediction.
    • Clause 3.2.3 Where good strike data is not available, consider the potential risk from the existence of wildlife and their movements on and in the vicinity of the aerodrome.

    Calculator choice

    This is why the calculator lets you enter presence only, strikes only, or both. It tells you which evidence a rating rests on, and it adds a note when strike data are missing.

    3.3 Numbers or ratings

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.2.6 Data can be quantitative or qualitative, depending on the resources of the aerodrome. Best practice is to use quantitative data.
    • Clause 3.2.7 Both kinds are used to rank how likely a species is to be involved in a strike, because aerodromes differ in the quality and quantity of information they hold.

    3.5 Behaviour can move the rating

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.2.9 Different biology and behaviour can place a species in a different level:
      • species that shy away from aircraft noise or learn to avoid aircraft could be rated low probability;
      • birds that flock in large numbers to certain habitats in the flight path could be rated high or very high;
      • solitary animals might be rated moderate;
      • species with low or erratic flights could be rated high or very high;
      • species active at night, on aerodromes with night flights, should have a higher probability of impact.
    • Clause 3.2.10 Probability can also vary with the season, the age or gender of the animals, and conditions such as grass length, rain and other weather.
    • Clause 3.2.11 More flight operations mean a higher probability of a strike, and larger, faster aircraft are likely to increase the risk. Relating the number of impacts to the number of operations shows whether rising impacts come from more traffic, more wildlife, or both.

    Calculator choice

    The calculator cannot see behaviour, season or traffic. You apply them yourself with the professional judgement override and a short reason, which is kept in the register. The Clause 3.4.4 case study does exactly this (section 7).

    3.6 How the calculator decides

    In plain words

    1. Each piece of evidence you enter is turned into a level using the bands above.
    2. If you entered more than one, the highest level is used. This is the cautious reading.
    3. If you use the judgement override, your level replaces the automatic one and both are shown.

    4 Step 4: severity

    In plain words

    Severity is how much harm a strike by this species would reasonably cause. Big animals and big flocks do more damage. You can judge it from what has actually happened at your aerodrome, or, if you have no damage history, from the animal's weight and how it flocks.

    4.1 What severity means

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.3.1 Rank the expected severity of the impact or damage from a strike, using a scale similar to the probability scale.
    • Clause 3.3.2 Severity is the damage the animal caused after impact. For observed wildlife it depends essentially on the size of the animal and its tendency to flock or congregate. Heavier wildlife and larger flocks increase the probability of damaging the aircraft and affecting flight performance.
    • Clause 3.3.3 It can be rated in terms of aircraft damage and human casualty, strikes with a consequence of damage, and the number of events that affect flights, for example a missed approach or aborted take-off.

    4.2 From your own damage history (Tables 3-2 and 3-3)

    Doc 9137 says

    Table 3-2 shows two ways to read a species' damage record, using the common kestrel as the example: the share of its strikes that caused damage, and the type of damage.

    LevelShare of the species' strikes that caused damageDamage type

    Damage types in Table 3-3

    • Catastrophic: equipment destroyed and multiple deaths.
    • Hazardous: a large reduction in safety margins, serious injury and major equipment damage.
    • Major: a significant reduction in safety margins, a serious incident, injury to persons.
    • Minor: nuisance, operating limitations, use of emergency procedures, a minor incident.
    • Negligible: no safety consequences, no aircraft damage, a near miss.

    4.3 With no damage history: mass times flock

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.3.5 Where there is no data on the damage a species can cause, severity can be calculated as the mass multiplied by the type of flock.
    • Clause 3.3.6 The categories of mass and flock size should be established by the aerodrome operator so each species fits a category.

    In plain words

    Pigeons (mass value 8) in loose flocks (value 2) score 8 x 2 = 16, which is High. A solitary sparrow scores 2 x 1 = 2, which is Very low. A big bird of prey (32) that is solitary (1) scores 32, which is Very high.

    4.4 Make it fit your aerodrome

    Doc 9137 says

    Clause 3.3.10 Each aerodrome should determine its own severity scale. Because the damage of a collision also depends on the type of aircraft, the range of aircraft sizes and types operating at the aerodrome must be taken into account, and the views of the aircraft operators should be considered.

    Calculator choice

    • The calculator offers only the five example mass bands and three flock values from Doc 9137. For an animal between two bands, choose the nearer one, or use the override and say why.
    • If you enter a damage percentage or a worst damage type, those are treated as additional evidence. The highest of all the severity evidence is used, and you can override it.

    5 Step 5: the matrix and the risk level

    In plain words

    Put probability on one axis and severity on the other. Where a species lands tells you how urgently to act. Doc 9137 groups the result into three levels: acceptable, tolerable, intolerable.

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.1.7 The most common form of assessment sorts probability and severity into levels, usually very low, low, moderate, high and very high, in a double-entry matrix in which species are classified.
    • Clause 3.4.1 The matrix is completed by combining the probability and severity of each species to decide whether further action is required. The assessment should be reviewed at least annually, or after a significant wildlife strike event, and the control measures adjusted.
    • Clause 3.4.3 Level 1 (Green), acceptable: no further action is required. Level 2 (Yellow), tolerable: the risk can be tolerated based on the mitigation in place; review current action and identify possible further action. Level 3 (Red), intolerable: take immediate action; further action is required to reduce the risk.

    Calculator choice: how a cell gets its colour

    The extracted Doc 9137 matrix does not state which cells are green, yellow or red. The calculator uses a simple rule that reproduces the Chapter 3 matrix and the Clause 3.4.4 case study:

    1. Score = (probability level + 1) x (severity level + 1), where Very low is 1 and Very high is 5. The score runs from 1 to 25.
    2. Red if the score is 15 or more. Yellow if 8 to 14. Green if 7 or less.
    3. A Very high severity is never rated below yellow, however unlikely the strike.

    Each aerodrome sets its own allocation in its WHMP. The grid below is generated by the same code that runs the calculator.

    7 Example B: the Clause 3.4.4 case study

    Doc 9137 says (Clause 3.4.4, generic data)

    • Common kestrel: a common resident that caused nine impacts, with minor damage in two. Probability is rated High. By the damage record severity would be Very high, but the species is typically solitary and weighs under 300 g, and the damage was always minor, so severity is reduced to Moderate. Conclusion: apply more mitigation, or improve what exists, to lower the probability of impact.
    • Barn swallow: a migratory species that caused many impacts in spring and summer, number unknown. Probability is rated High, though being seasonal could allow some reduction. Severity is Very low: no damage and a body mass of about 20 g. Conclusion: the presence is acceptable, but keep applying the measures already in place.
    • Griffon vulture: appeared for the first time for several days in the year, with no history. Probability is rated High because of the new presence and its persistence. Severity is Very high because of its size (over 7 kg) and flight form. Conclusion: mitigation must be applied to minimize or eliminate its presence before impacts occur.

    How the calculator reproduces it

    The table shows the inputs used and the live result. Press Case study in the calculator to load the same three species, including the judgement overrides and their reasons.

    8 After the result: what to do

    Doc 9137 says

    • Clause 3.1.8 Use the matrix results to prioritize wildlife management techniques and methods, and document the actions in the WHMP (Chapter 9).
    • Clause 9.1.4.3 Prioritize wildlife management by level of risk. Apply mitigation first to the species with the highest risk.
    • Clause 9.1.5.5 Each mitigation measure should have a performance indicator, so you can tell whether it works.
    • Clause 3.4.1 Re-assess at least annually, or after a significant strike, and adjust the measures.

    In plain words

    • Red: start with the cause. Remove what attracts the species (Chapter 4), then plan varied, manually operated dispersal (Chapter 5).
    • Yellow: check whether the current measures work, and look for one more measure that lowers probability.
    • Green: keep recording and keep what you have.

    9 What the calculator chooses for you, and where it stops

    Calculator choices

    1. Band limits. Shared limits take the higher band, gaps between printed ranges take the lower band, and the Low and Very low presence limits are read as 10 to 50 and under 10 days.
    2. Highest evidence wins. When several indicators are entered, the highest level is used as the cautious reading.
    3. Ratings map to levels. The qualitative columns of Table 3-1 are mapped in order, from Occasional or Rare (Very low) up to Permanent or Very often (Very high).
    4. Colour rule. The cell colouring rule in section 5 is illustrative.
    5. Example scales only. The mass and flock values are the example values in Tables 3-4 and 3-5.

    Not covered: the calculator does not know your aircraft types, traffic, seasons or the behaviour of a species, so it cannot apply Clauses 3.2.9 to 3.2.11 and 3.3.10 by itself. A real assessment needs competent personnel (Clause 3.2.4), your own data and the views of aircraft operators. Use the calculator to learn the method, screen species and organize your thinking.
